A community college. A lake. A small city where everybody knows somebody. A parking lot full of cars that have no reason to still be there.

This is New Baltimore, Michigan in 2015.

Not a fictionalized version of some vague Midwestern "small town." Not a rural nowhere. Not a generic college campus dropped into the woods.

New Baltimore sits on Anchor Bay at the northern end of Lake St. Clair, inside the Metro Detroit orbit. The lake is part of the town's daily geography. Downtown is compact. Houses, storefronts, parks, roads and the waterfront all overlap. People recognize each other. Students see their classmates outside school. Parents know other parents. Somebody's cousin works somewhere you just walked into.

ABCC sits inside that ecosystem.

The college is only one place people go.

After class, people drive.

Sometimes they have somewhere to be.

Usually they don't.

THE WORLD

SO, WHERE THE HELL ARE WE?

New Baltimore is a small city on Anchor Bay, part of Lake St. Clair in Macomb County, Michigan. It sits northeast of Detroit and within the larger suburban network of Metro Detroit.

The city is old enough to have an actual downtown and waterfront history, but suburban enough that the car is still the basic unit of movement. The lake isn't some exotic destination people make a special trip to see. It's simply there.

You drive toward it.

You park near it.

You walk along it.

You sit on a dock because nobody has anything else to do.

You go to the festival because everyone else is going.

You end up in someone's car after class and eventually somebody says they might as well head toward the water.

That's the social geography of the place.

Downtown isn't a polished tourist district. It's a working, lived-in part of the city where businesses, houses, streets, parks and the waterfront sit close together. Washington Street forms the main spine of the older downtown. Front Street runs toward the waterfront.

This physical closeness matters.

A student can leave ABCC, run into a classmate downtown, grab something to eat, walk toward the water, see somebody else's parents, and wind up sitting on the dock with three people they didn't plan on seeing that day.

Nobody thinks this is remarkable.

That's the point.

01

SMALL DOESN'T MEAN RURAL

New Baltimore is a waterfront suburb inside the Metro Detroit orbit. There are subdivisions, commercial corridors, traffic, chain stores, older homes, local businesses, marinas and major highways.

02

EVERYBODY OVERLAPS

School life doesn't stay at school. Classmates exist at the lake, downtown, at restaurants, at gas stations, at parties, at friends' houses and in the same parking lots.

03

THE LAKE IS BACKGROUND

Anchor Bay should ground scenes without hijacking them. You don't need a dramatic lake scene every time the characters are nearby. Sometimes it's just wind, boats, wet pavement or light on the water.

04

THE CAR IS A ROOM

Driving is social space. Conversations happen in parked cars. People listen to music. Windows come down. Someone gets distracted and takes the long way home. A car can be private without being isolated.

ABCC

ANCHOR BAY COMMUNITY COLLEGE

The school is important because people spend time there. It isn't important because the universe revolves around it.

ABCC is a small community college serving the surrounding area. Its social scale is completely different from a giant university.

Classes are small.

You see the same people.

You recognize professors.

Somebody knows somebody who knows you.

The result is less "college movie" and more everyone keeps accidentally becoming familiar.

SCHOOL IDENTITY

THE TARS

ABCC's school identity is the Tars. School colors are red and white.

School spirit exists, but this isn't a football movie. It's the kind of school identity that shows up on hoodies, gym clothes, flyers, athletic gear, banners and whatever somebody happened to buy at the bookstore.

CLASSROOMS

SMALL ROOMS

Think desks, not lecture-hall anonymity.

Students notice who sits where. Professors notice who showed up. People develop little routines without ever officially deciding that they're routines.

SOCIAL LIFE

THE CAMPUS IS ONLY THE START

People leave.

Someone says they're getting food. Somebody else needs gas. Somebody wants to go downtown. Somebody wants to go to the lake. Somebody says to just get in the car.

Then the afternoon becomes something else.

ACADEMICS

Roman is perfectly capable of passing his classes. His biggest academic problem is that there are approximately twelve more interesting things happening around him at any given moment.

PARKING LOT

THE UNOFFICIAL COMMON ROOM

The parking lot matters because cars matter.

People sit in them before class. After class. During breaks. While waiting for someone. While pretending they're about to leave.

SOCIAL RULE

This is a small community. Not every encounter is important. Most aren't.

That's what makes them useful.

PLACES

The world outside the classroom.

01
DOWNTOWN

NEW BALTIMORE

Downtown is the old core of the city.

Older buildings sit close to the sidewalk. Businesses, homes, streets and the waterfront occupy the same compact space. It is walkable without pretending to be some curated little tourist village.

It feels lived in.

There are errands.

Restaurants.

Local businesses.

People who recognize each other.

Students who came downtown for one thing and somehow ended up doing something else.

DOWNTOWN IS THE COLLISION ZONE.

It is where school life and local life constantly run into each other.

02
MAIN STREET / DOWNTOWN SPINE

WASHINGTON STREET

Washington Street is the everyday downtown artery.

Not glamorous.

Not a movie set.

Just town.

Older buildings and local businesses create a tighter, more enclosed feeling than the open waterfront.

Students walk it. Parents run errands. Locals recognize people. Somebody pulls over because they know somebody.

Roman is particularly bad at being anonymous here.

He knows someone.

Someone knows him.

Somebody says his name from across the street.

NOTHING HERE NEEDS TO BE A PLOT.

A random encounter is enough.

03
WATERFRONT

FRONT STREET

Front Street is the transition between downtown and the water.

Washington Street feels enclosed and urban.

Front Street opens up.

You get boats.

Water.

Wind.

More sky.

It is one of the natural places for a scene to decompress without announcing that it is becoming an Important Scene.

Somebody can park.

Somebody can walk.

Somebody can stare at the water while the conversation keeps going.

END UP AT THE WATER.
04
WATERFRONT / ANCHOR BAY

WALTER & MARY BURKE PARK

Burke Park is the town's living room.

It is a public waterfront space where the city and the bay meet.

Beach. Boardwalk. Picnic areas. Fishing. Swimming. Playground. Pavilion. Volleyball. Benches. Dock.

The dock extends into the bay.

The park is not magically empty because two characters need privacy. It is a real public place. Families are around. Teenagers are around. Fishermen are around. People are walking.

Sometimes there are simply people sitting there because they don't have anything better to do.

During major events, it becomes even more crowded.

06
LAKESHORE ROAD

M-29

M-29 is the road you take when you want to keep moving without necessarily wanting to go somewhere.

It follows the lakeshore through the broader area, connecting stretches of residential streets, businesses and glimpses of the water.

At night, streetlights pass across the windshield.

The road is familiar enough that somebody can drive it without thinking.

That makes it useful.

A car can become its own little room while the world moves past outside.

NO DESTINATION REQUIRED.
07
INTERSTATE

I-94

I-94 is not scenic.

It is infrastructure.

Concrete. Signs. Traffic. Billboards. Headlights. Tire noise.

It connects New Baltimore to the larger Metro Detroit region and toward Port Huron.

It is what you use when you're actually going somewhere.

It is also what you use when you need to get the hell out of town for a while.

08
SUBURBAN FRINGE

23 / 24 MILE ROAD

The farther you move toward the broader commercial corridors, the less distinct the old waterfront town feels.

Wide roads.

Big parking lots.

Chain stores.

Fast food.

Newer subdivisions.

Construction.

This is the suburban infrastructure surrounding New Baltimore's older core.

It's where you go when you need something practical.

It isn't where the town feels most like itself.

09
NEIGHBORHOODS

THE RESIDENTIAL STREETS

New Baltimore's residential areas aren't one uniform subdivision.

There are older houses closer to the established downtown fabric and newer suburban developments farther out.

Some streets are busy.

Some are quiet.

Some dead-end into little wooded pockets where teenagers can park without feeling like the entire town is watching.

These places matter because not every private moment needs to happen somewhere dramatic.

SOMETIMES IT'S JUST A CAR AT THE END OF A ROAD.
10
SPORTS / RESIDENTIAL EDGE

FESTIVAL PARK

Festival Park is different from the waterfront.

Less downtown. Less spectacle. More open suburban space.

In the 2015 setting, it has the feeling of a community project still taking shape: grassy fields, baseball diamonds, fencing, gravel and parking, youth sports, families and the general sound of people doing normal suburban things.

It is a good place for Roman and his friends because nobody needs to explain why they are there.

They can just be there.

UNIVERSE RULES

THIS IS A SANDBOX.

01

NO CHOSEN ONE.

Nobody is secretly destined to save the town. There is no prophecy. There is no supernatural conspiracy underneath ABCC.

02

NO FORCED PLOT.

A scene can simply be a scene. Characters can hang out, talk, argue, flirt, drive, eat, get bored, leave and come back.

03

PEOPLE HAVE THEIR OWN LIVES.

Characters can text other people. Make plans. Get distracted. Go somewhere else. Leave a room. Notice something happening across campus.

04

NOT EVERYTHING IS CINEMATIC.

Most moments are mundane.

Mundane is good.

05

THE WORLD CONTINUES WITHOUT YOU.

People have conversations you aren't part of. They know things you don't. They make plans that don't involve you.

06

SHORT INPUTS SHOULD STILL WORK.

A character saying "let's go" can become a drive. "Where?" can become a disagreement. "I'm hungry" can become a trip somewhere.

07

LOCAL DETAILS SHOULD BE CASUAL.

Don't dump geography into dialogue. Let geography exist underneath the scene.

08

THE LAKE DOES NOT HAVE TO BE THE POINT.

Sometimes the characters are by the lake. Sometimes they aren't. The world doesn't need to announce it.
